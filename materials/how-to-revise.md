# How to revise: Belinda's method

From Jack's session with Belinda on 3 November 2026. Use this for any subject, any test.

## The big idea

Belinda: "But what will surprise you is that they can only test you on subjects that you have studied. So it's just a revision. It's just a little bit of recapping, looking through your workbooks to see what you've covered and just reminding yourself."

Belinda: "You know, whatever's in your science book, that's what you need to revise. And that's just by reading over, reading each chapter in your science book, what's here, and then just taking notes, do a mind map of what you can remember. And you just do that over and over. You know, don't worry about looking at the stuff you haven't covered. It's all here. It's all in your book. You know? If it's not in your books, then it's not worth looking at."

So: your exercise book is your revision guide. You have already done the work. Revision is bringing it back to the front of your mind, not learning it all again.

## The method, one section at a time

1. **Pick one section.** Use a subheading from your book, for example "Energy and temperature" or "Atomic models". One section, not a whole topic.
2. **Brain dump first.** Close the book. Write down everything you remember and draw the diagrams you remember. It doesn't matter if it's messy or short.
3. **Read the section in your book.** Read it, then read it again. Look at your diagrams.
4. **Make notes again.** Write a revision card or a page: key words, a labelled diagram, any formula. In biology, label everything.
5. **Fill the gaps, and only the gaps.** If something in your book doesn't make sense or is missing, look up just that bit: CGP book, Kerboodle, BBC Bitesize, Seneca. Add what you find to your notes.
6. **Let the AI study notebook test you.** Ask it to quiz you and to tell you what you've missed. Don't ask it to write your notes for you (see below).
7. **Tick it off and come back later.** In a few days, brain dump the same section again. Whatever you've forgotten is what you read next.

Belinda: "Anything you're not sure about, just look at the bit that you're not sure about, don't take yourself right back to the beginning."

## Short bursts, not long sessions

Belinda: "Give yourself five, ten minutes, then get up, walk about. Come back, do another 10 minutes, you know what I mean? I said, people have got to know themselves and follow the way you work naturally."

One section is about one or two short bursts. Two or three sections a day is plenty.

Belinda: "Don't overdo it. Because if you make yourself overtired, nothing's going to go in."

## Homework counts

Belinda: "And every time you do a piece of homework, that's like revision. Because it means you're going over something again, you know, it's all here."

Keeping up with homework is already part of revising. When homework and revision clash, do the homework and treat it as revision for that topic.

## Read the question, then give yourself a minute

Belinda: "Sometimes you have to take your time to read the question and take your time before you can click anything to think about the answer. Because half the time you'll know it. But you've just got to give yourself a minute to get there."

In a test: read the question twice, underline what it asks for, then answer. If you're stuck, move on and come back. A question you skip and come back to is better than a rushed wrong answer.

## When several tests come at once

Write a list for each test. Use the learning criteria at the front of your book, or the topic list your teacher gave you.

Then do the earliest test first, two or three sections a day, in short bursts. You don't need to finish everything to do well. Every section you go over is one more you can answer.

## Using AI the right way

AI is a navigator, not an answer machine. It should point you to the right page and ask you questions. If it writes your notes or summarises the chapter for you, you skip the bit that makes you remember.

Good things to ask the study notebook:

- "Ask me five questions on isotopes from my sources, one at a time. Don't tell me the answer until I've tried."
- "Here is my brain dump on atomic models. What have I missed? Don't fill it in, just tell me what to look up."
- "Which page in my sources explains how to work out the number of neutrons?"
- "Which topics on my chemistry list have I not made notes on yet?"

Things not to ask:

- "Write my notes on the particle model."
- "Summarise chapter 2 for me."
- "What's the answer to question 4?"

## The resources you already have

Belinda: "Because if you start to look in too many different areas, it just becomes overwhelming. You've got the knowledge, you've got the knowledge and you've got the resources already."

In order: your exercise books, then Kerboodle and your CGP books, then BBC Bitesize, Seneca, Cognito or Free Science Lessons for gaps. All the links are on the [Resources](resources.html) page.
