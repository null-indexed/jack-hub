Jack's Learning Hub: study pack for AI study guides

Last updated: 5 November 2026. Source: https://null-indexed.github.io/jack-hub/

This is a plain text version of Jack's Learning Hub for an AI study guide (Gemini, NotebookLM). The hub's home page is built with JavaScript, which AI readers can't see, so read this instead. Jack is in Year 9 (2026 to 2027). His GCSEs are in May and June 2028.

How to use this pack (for the AI)

  • Revise only what is in Jack's exercise books. The tests cover what has been taught.
  • Ask one question at a time. Never stack questions.
  • If Jack is stuck, give him the first step or a sentence with a gap, not the whole answer. Help him find the answer in his book or on a hub sheet rather than giving it.
  • Keep each go short, about 10 minutes, then suggest a break. About 20 minutes a day in total, after homework. Do not use countdown timers.
  • Traffic lights: red = I don't know it, amber = I kind of know it, green = I know it. Only use colours Jack gives you. Never guess them. Do ambers first, then small pieces of reds. Greens only need a look the night before.
  • Start each topic with recall: ask Jack what he remembers with his book closed, then check.
  • Link to things Jack likes where it helps: engines, batteries, building things.
  • Never do homework or Sparx for him, and never write whole topic summaries.
  • Be calm and specific. Point to what he got right ("you worked out the neutrons correctly") rather than general praise.
  • If this pack and a teacher's sheet disagree, the teacher's sheet wins.

Part 2: What each test covers

How to read this part: "In the test" means it is on the teacher's checklist and has been taught. "Ask the teacher" means it is on the checklist but may not have been taught yet. Only revise what is in Jack's exercise books.

Chemistry (OCR Gateway Chemistry J248), Tuesday 10 November

From the teacher's checklist "PLC Single Chemistry unit 1-3 paper 1". The checklist lists all of Paper 1 (C1 to C3), which is the whole GCSE paper, not this test. The test covers what has been taught, which looks like C1.

In the test, C1.1 Particle model:

  • Describe the main features of the particle model in terms of states of matter and change of state.
  • Explain, in terms of the particle model, the difference between physical changes and chemical changes.
  • Higher tier only: discuss the limits of the particle model when particles are shown as hard spheres.

In the test, C1.2 Atomic structure (the main focus):

  • Describe how and why the atomic model has changed over time.
  • Describe the structure of the atom and name the subatomic particles.
  • State the approximate size of atoms, the relative size of the nucleus, and where most of the mass is.
  • State the relative charges of protons, neutrons and electrons, and the overall charge of an atom.
  • State the relative masses of protons, neutrons and electrons, and how mass is spread in an atom.
  • Calculate the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons from the atomic number and mass number.
  • Define atomic number and mass number.
  • Define an ion and an isotope and use standard notation for them.

Ask the teacher (done in class, may be in): the periodic table bits covered in class, such as the halogens and how electrons are arranged in shells; balancing equations and catalysts.

Not in this test unless the teacher says so: C2.1 purity and separating mixtures, C2.2 bonding and dot and cross diagrams, C2.3 carbon structures, C3 moles and quantitative chemistry, acids.

Physics (OCR Gateway Physics J249), Monday 9 or Friday 13 November

From the teacher's checklist "PLC Single Physics unit 1-4 paper 1". The checklist covers P1 to P4; the test is expected to cover P1 Matter. The school's curriculum map puts P1.2 in spring and P1.3 in summer, so check Jack's exercise book for which parts have been taught, and ask the teacher if unsure.

P1.1 Particles and density:

  • Describe how and why the atomic model has changed over time.
  • Describe the structure of the atom, the charges and relative sizes of the particles.
  • State the typical size of atoms and small molecules.
  • Define density.
  • Explain why solids, liquids and gases have different densities, using the arrangement of particles.
  • Use density = mass ÷ volume, including when mass stays the same.
  • Practical: measuring the volume of a regular solid (length × width × height) and an irregular one (displacement can).

P1.2 Changes of state and heating:

  • Describe how mass is conserved when substances melt, freeze, evaporate, condense or sublimate.
  • Physical changes can be reversed and the material gets back its original properties; chemical changes cannot.
  • Describe how heating changes the energy stored in a system and raises its temperature or changes its state.
  • Define specific heat capacity and say how it differs from specific latent heat.
  • Use change in internal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × change in temperature.
  • Use energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat.
  • Read heating and cooling graphs: the flat parts are where the state is changing.

P1.3 Gas pressure:

  • Explain how the motion of gas particles relates to the gas's temperature and pressure.
  • At constant volume, hotter gas means higher pressure (describe it, no calculation).
  • Gases can be squashed or expanded by pressure changes; pressure pushes at right angles to any surface.
  • Physics only: bigger volume at the same temperature means lower pressure; atmospheric pressure and height. Higher tier only: work done on a gas raises its temperature; pressure in liquids, floating and sinking.

Biology (OCR Gateway Biology J247), Wednesday 11 or Thursday 12 November

From the teacher's checklist "PLC Single Biology unit 1-3 paper 1". The test is expected to cover B1.1 and B1.2. B1.3 respiration and B1.4 photosynthesis come later in the year.

B1.1 Cells:

  • Describe how light microscopes and staining are used to view cells.
  • Name the main parts of eukaryotic cells (animal and plant) and prokaryotic cells (bacteria) and say what each part does.
  • Explain how electron microscopes have helped us understand the parts of cells.

B1.2 DNA and enzymes:

  • Describe DNA as a polymer made of two strands forming a double helix.
  • Describe DNA as made from four different nucleotides. Each nucleotide is a phosphate, a sugar and a base.
  • Complementary base pairing: A pairs with T, C pairs with G.
  • Describe experiments that investigate enzyme reactions (practical PAG 4: amylase breaking down starch, tested with iodine; time how long until the starch has gone).
  • Explain how enzymes work: lock and key, active site, substrate, denaturing by heat or the wrong pH, optimum temperature.
  • Rate of reaction = 1 ÷ time taken.
  • Separate biology and higher tier only: protein synthesis and the triplet code.

Question types Jack has met on the B1.2 test paper: DNA structure (multiple choice), lock and key (what the key stands for), circling one nucleotide on a DNA diagram and labelling a sugar, explaining "polymer", describing how an enzyme works, working out a rate from a time, and a standard form calculation.

Part 3: Key facts and formulas (plain text)

  • density = mass ÷ volume (units: kg/m³ or g/cm³)
  • volume of a cuboid = length × width × height
  • change in internal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × change in temperature
  • energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat
  • number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number
  • in a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons = atomic number
  • proton: relative charge +1, relative mass 1. neutron: charge 0, mass 1. electron: charge −1, mass very small (about 1/2000).
  • electron shells fill 2, then 8, then 8. An atom that loses an electron becomes a positive ion; one that gains an electron becomes a negative ion.
  • rate of reaction = 1 ÷ time taken
  • DNA base pairs: A with T, C with G
  • Pythagoras: a² + b² = c², where c is the longest side
  • percentage of an amount: divide by 100, then multiply by the percentage
  • negative × negative = positive; negative × positive = negative
