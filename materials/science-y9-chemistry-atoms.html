Chemistry: atoms, the periodic table and atomic models

OCR Gateway Chemistry (J248), Year 9. Built from Jack's session with Belinda on 3 November 2026. Use it with Belinda's method: book first, brain dump, then check.

What you need to be able to do

  • Use the periodic table, not just have it in front of you: say what each number means and work things out from them
  • Count the atoms of each element in a formula
  • Describe how the model of the atom changed over time, and why each model was replaced
  • Explain what isotopes are
  • Say what the halogens are and how they behave as you go down the group

The periodic table: what do the numbers mean?

Belinda: "There's no point just having the periodic table if you don't know how to use it."

Each element's box has two numbers. Find them for carbon, oxygen and sodium, then answer from your book or Bitesize:

  1. Which number is the atomic number, and what does it tell you?
  2. Which number is the mass number, and what does it tell you?
  3. How do you work out the number of neutrons from the two numbers?
  4. In a neutral atom, how does the number of electrons compare with the number of protons? Why?

Counting atoms in a formula

The method:

  • Every element symbol starts with a capital letter (O, Na, Cl). A small letter belongs to the capital before it.
  • A small number written low after a symbol tells you how many of that atom. No number means one.
  • The same element can appear in more than one place in a formula. Count every place and add them up.

This last rule is the trap. In the session, the ethanol question (C₂H₅OH) was the one that caught us out: hydrogen appears twice.

Try these. For each one, write the number of atoms of each element and the total.

  1. NH₃ (ammonia)
  2. CO₂
  3. H₂SO₄
  4. C₂H₅OH (ethanol)
  5. CH₃COOH
  6. Ca(OH)₂ (hint: look up what the number after a bracket does)

Questions to answer under your table:

  1. In the gold foil experiment, most alpha particles went straight through and a few bounced back. What did each of those results tell Rutherford?
  2. Why did the plum pudding model have to be replaced?
  3. Why is it fine for a scientific model to be replaced? What does that tell you about how science works?

Check your book for which models your teacher covered. If Schrödinger isn't in your book, it's extra: nice to know, not a priority.

Isotopes

Skim the isotopes page in your book or Kerboodle and make a short note card. Your card should answer:

  1. What is the same and what is different between two isotopes of the same element?
  2. Why do isotopes of an element react in the same way?
  3. Carbon-12 and carbon-14: how many protons and neutrons does each have?

Halogens (Group 7)

You already know some of this from lessons, so brain dump first. Then check:

  1. Which group are the halogens in, and why does that matter for how they react?
  2. Name the first four halogens and say what state each is at room temperature.
  3. What happens to reactivity as you go down the group?
  4. Use the pattern to predict something about astatine, at the bottom of the group.

Self-test questions

  1. An atom has atomic number 11 and mass number 23. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does it have? Which element is it?
  2. Why is the mass number never smaller than the atomic number?
  3. Describe one way Bohr's model improved on Rutherford's.
  4. How many atoms in total are in one molecule of H₂SO₄?
  5. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 are isotopes. What is the difference between them?
