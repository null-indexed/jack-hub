Biology: DNA, nucleotides and base pairs

OCR Gateway Biology (J247), Year 9, topic 1.2. Made on 4 November 2026 after the DNA exam questions. Use it with Belinda's method: book first, brain dump, then check.

Step 1: brain dump

Close everything. Draw DNA as well as you can and write every word you know about it. Two minutes. Then read on and see what you already had. You knew more than you thought on 4 November: you knew a nucleotide is the building block of DNA and that it has a spiral shape. That's the right start.

The whole thing in three sizes

Think of it like Lego. Small piece, then a chain of pieces, then two chains twisted together.

  1. Small: a nucleotide. One small unit.
  2. Medium: lots of nucleotides joined in a long chain make one strand. A long molecule made of repeating units is called a polymer, so DNA is a polymer of nucleotides.
  3. Large: two strands joined in the middle and twisted make the double helix.

What one nucleotide looks like

Every nucleotide has three parts:

  • a phosphate group
  • a sugar called deoxyribose (the D in DNA, which stands for deoxyribonucleic acid)
  • one base

The phosphate and the sugar are the same in every nucleotide. Only the base changes, and there are four bases:

A: adenineT: thymineC: cytosineG: guanine

So there are four kinds of nucleotide, one for each base. That's what the spec means by "a polymer made from four different nucleotides".

How the nucleotides fit together

The phosphate of one nucleotide joins to the sugar of the next, over and over, making a long strand. That chain of sugar, phosphate, sugar, phosphate is called the sugar phosphate backbone. It's the side of the ladder.

The bases stick out sideways. Two strands line up with their bases facing each other, and each base joins to the one opposite. Each rung of the ladder is a base pair.

Where to draw the circle (the question from 4 November). One nucleotide is one phosphate, one sugar and one base, all on the same side. So you circle half a rung plus the bit of backbone it's attached to, like the teal box on the diagram. A base pair is two bases, one from each strand, so every rung is made from two different nucleotides. "Half of it" was the right idea, as long as you include the sugar and phosphate on that side.

Why the bases pair up

The pairs are always the same:

  • A pairs with T
  • C pairs with G

This is called complementary base pairing. A memory trick: Apple in the Tree, Car in the Garage.

The reason is shape, not "opposites attract" like magnets. A and T are the right shape to fit together, and so are C and G, a bit like jigsaw pieces. Where they fit, weak bonds called hydrogen bonds form between them and hold the two strands together. A and T are held by two of these bonds and C and G by three, which may be the "three" you spotted on the diagram on your sheet. Check your book to see whether you need the numbers; the pairing rule is the part that always comes up.

Why weak bonds are a good thing: the cell has to unzip DNA to copy it and to read genes. Weak bonds in the middle let the strands come apart easily, while the strong backbone keeps each strand in one piece.

The twist

Why the order of bases matters

The order of the bases along one strand is the genetic code. Read in groups of three bases (a triplet), each group codes for one amino acid. Amino acids join in a chain and fold up to make a protein. A gene is a section of DNA that codes for one protein. If the "three" on your sheet was about the code rather than the bonds, this is it.

How far your class went into making proteins (words like mRNA, ribosome, transcription and translation) is in your book. Only revise the bits you covered.

Link back to Biology 1.1: in plant and animal cells, DNA is in the nucleus, in chromosomes. In bacteria there is no nucleus, so the DNA is a loop in the cytoplasm, plus small rings called plasmids.
