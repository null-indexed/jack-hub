Chemical reactions

In a chemical reaction, the atoms in the reactants rearrange to make new substances, the products. No atoms are made or destroyed. They just swap partners.

Signs that a reaction has happened: a colour change, fizzing (a gas is made), a temperature change, or a solid appearing in a liquid (a precipitate).

Because no atoms are lost, the total mass stays the same. This is called conservation of mass. If a reaction seems to lose mass, it's usually because a gas has escaped.

Two ways to write a reaction:

Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
Symbol equation: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

State symbols, if your class uses them: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.

Balancing equations

A balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides. It's counting, the same skill as counting atoms in a formula from the atoms sheet.

The one rule: you may only change the big numbers in front. Never change the small numbers, because that would change the substance (H₂O is water, but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide).

Method:

  1. Write the unbalanced equation.
  2. Draw a little table: each element, how many on the left, how many on the right.
  3. Pick an element that doesn't match. Put a big number in front of a formula to fix it.
  4. Recount everything (the big number multiplies every atom in that formula).
  5. Repeat until every row matches.

Worked example: hydrogen + oxygen → water

EquationH left / rightO left / right
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O2 / 22 / 1✗
H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O2 / 42 / 2✗
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O4 / 42 / 2✓

Fixing one element can unbalance another. That's normal. Just keep recounting. Like Belinda says, give yourself a minute.

Catalysts

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. At the end you have the same amount of catalyst you started with, so it can be used again.

How: every reaction needs a push of energy to get started, called the activation energy. A catalyst gives the reaction a different route that needs less energy, so more particles have enough energy to react.

Example you may have seen: manganese dioxide makes hydrogen peroxide break down into water and oxygen much faster.

Enzymes: catalysts in living things

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up reactions in your body, like digesting food, and they're proteins, so they're made from instructions in your DNA. That links straight back to the DNA sheet.

  • The molecule an enzyme works on is the substrate.
  • It fits into a dip on the enzyme called the active site, like a key in a lock.
  • Each enzyme only fits one substrate. This is called being specific.
  • Too hot, or the wrong pH, and the active site changes shape. The substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured, and that can't be undone.
  • Each enzyme works best at one temperature and one pH (its optimum). For enzymes in the human body, the best temperature is about 37°C.
